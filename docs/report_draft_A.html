Naming an unknown molecule from its mass spectra

How the current public model works, and what our first strategy session taught us  ·  October 18, 2026

Kaggle “Enveda CASMI 2026 – Molecule ID From Mass Spectra”, Sep 14 – Dec 14, 2026

1The problem

A tandem mass spectrometer weighs a charged molecule (the precursor ion), shatters it by gas collisions and weighs the pieces. A spectrum lists the resulting peaks as (fragment m/z, intensity), m/z being mass over charge. For each of ~400 hidden natural-product-like molecules we get 1–16 spectra (median 3; Bruker timsTOF; 20/40/60 eV; ten possible adducts) and must return up to 25 ranked structures (SMILES).

  • Score, MRR@25: 1/rank of the first correct guess (rank 4 → 0.25; beyond 25 → 0), averaged. Correct = same atom connectivity (InChIKey first block; stereo ignored).
  • Noisy board: the public leaderboard uses ~124 molecules (31%); one molecule going from miss to rank 1 moves it ~0.008 (standard error ~0.016).
  • Data and rules: 2.54M training spectra of ~275,810 structures from 11 public libraries; offline notebook, ≤ 9 h; prizes need public, free data (no NIST) and reproducible weights.
  • Standing (Oct 17): top 0.495; best public notebooks ~0.44–0.45; 3,486 teams.

Jargon in plain words

Adduct – the charged form measured, e.g. [M+H]+ = molecule + proton; it fixes M from the precursor m/z.

Fragment – a piece left after bonds break (one peak).

Isomers – different structures, same formula and mass.

Fingerprint – bits for substructures; Tanimoto = overlap.

Analog – a different molecule with a similar spectrum, e.g. the unknown minus one OH.

Forward model – predicts a structure’s spectrum.

Ranker – orders candidates (LightGBM, LambdaRank).

ppm – parts-per-million mass error.

2How the current public model works

The best public notebook (we forked dmitriigluzdov’s “From Spectra to Structures”) stacks many community contributions into one retrieve-then-rank pipeline (Fig. 1):

  1. Shortlist by mass. Candidates = pool structures within 10 ppm of M, mostly isomers, so the spectra must decide.
  2. Collect evidence (engine 1). Library match (spectral-entropy similarity to the candidate’s own reference spectra) solves class 1. Analog propagation: training spectra resembling the query (after a mass shift) vote for structurally similar candidates. FPNet, a transformer over peaks + metadata, predicts the fingerprint; candidates with agreeing bits score high. Fragmentation: how much peak intensity 1–2 bond breaks explain. Four LightGBM boosters combine ~100 features, plus a popularity bonus (log PubChem substance + literature counts).
  3. Look beyond the pool. Fingerprint models screen ~105M PubChem structures; a proposal jumps to rank 1 if its fingerprint margin is large (> 150) or it is well documented, unless a strong library match (similarity ≥ 0.9) exists.
  4. Second opinion, fusion, check. Engine 2 adds an independent list; fusion rewards candidates ranked high by either; forward models settle ties among isomers.

History: ~0.34 (mid-Sep) → 0.40 (engine fusion) → 0.44 (+ forward-model re-rank) → 0.45 (tuning).

Weak spots: almost no class-3 hits; many errors are the wrong same-formula isomer; most refinements were accepted on the noisy 124-molecule board without cross-validation; some bundled weights have uncertain licence or reproducibility (CC-BY-NC-SA; an unpublished ranker-row file), a prize risk.

3Our strategy: what we tried, what we learned

B — Class 3, the largest untapped share

We built a stand-in for the unseen novel molecules: matching training structures against all 106M PubChem connectivity keys left 16,917 measured molecules that are not in PubChem, our class-3 proxy. 45% of them split at one amide or ester bond into two PubChem-known pieces (N-acyl amino acids, bile-acid conjugates, fatty-acid esters). Our NovelEngine therefore emits only structures absent from PubChem, COCONUT and training, from two generators:

  • Analog derivatives: find similar training spectra by modified cosine (which also matches peaks shifted by the mass difference Δ) and apply edits (hydroxylation, methylation, glycosylation, chain extension, …) whose mass change is Δ. The top-20 analogs reach the answer for 24% of the proxy.
  • Conjugates: join a known acid and a known amine or alcohol (amide/ester/glycoside) whose masses add up; rank by diagnostic fragment ions of each half.

Our site-localisation score (Fig. 3) checks where an edit sits: derivative MRR on the proxy rose from 0.061 to 0.107. A learned LambdaRank ranker lifted proxy MRR from 0.124 to 0.269.

C — Bug hunt. A code review of the public pipeline found two train/serve mismatches (a feature computed one way in training, another at prediction time): (1) the “number of peaks” feature sees raw test peaks (median 230) but was trained on cleaned ones (median 52); (2) hidden-test positive-mode m/z carry an electron-mass correction (−0.000548 Da) missing from the enveda-180 training spectra. This shifts mass-error features by ~1.6 ppm at the median mass, versus a ~2.5 ppm typical gap between competing formulas. Two patches are pending.

The lesson we put centre stage

A board probe with novel candidates only scored 0.010 against ~0.045 forecast: the proxy overstated class-3 reach ~4–5×. The engine works (known-only probe: 0.085), but class 3 is worth at most ~0.01 as an add-on, about one molecule at rank 1. A proxy is a hypothesis about the test: validate it against the leaderboard early. As the board is noisy (±0.016), we also need offline validation that mimics the test (track D).

D — Compliance-first own pipeline. Prize entries need models reproducible from permitted data, so we build our own: a 726,198-structure pool (training + COCONUT), library, analog, fragment and COCONUT-documentation channels, and a dual encoder. One MLP embeds the spectrum (1-Da peak bins, neutral losses, adduct/energy/instrument), another the molecule (2,215 fingerprint bits); a contrastive loss pulls the true structure closer than up to 64 same-mass “hard negatives”. After one CPU epoch: MRR 0.51 (top-1 36%) among same-mass candidates on held-out structures. A LambdaRank ranker trains on simulated queries from 8,000 structures (class 1: hide the query’s spectra; class 2: hide all its spectra) and is validated on 1,500 disjoint ones, using the measured mass (the true one would leak the answer) and the electron correction.

E — Next. Compare variants on the board, finish the own pipeline (target ≥ 0.45, fully compliant), add class-3 candidates only where the main list is weak, and pick the two finals by validation + board. If the rules are enforced, strong compliant entries may outrank non-reproducible ones.
